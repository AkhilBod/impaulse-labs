Mandour & Associates: What Will Be Your Trademark? Scholarship
Prompt: When your career is over, what will you be known for? (500 words max, in email body)

I want to be known as the engineer whose software didn't break on people.

That sounds modest, and it is. Most software failures never make the news. They show up as someone losing an afternoon of work, getting charged twice, or watching a form erase everything they typed. They happen to people who don't have the time or energy to deal with it, and I grew up around that kind of exhaustion. My family has dealt with long health problems and money stress, including a move from Cupertino to Pennsylvania. I watched my parents fight with insurance portals and billing systems that seemed designed by someone who had never been tired.

So I care about the boring parts of engineering: what happens when a request fails, when two updates collide, when a user does something nobody expected.

I've started building that reputation already. I built InterviewSense, an AI interview practice platform that has grown to more than 25,000 users. Early on, users were losing credits when an AI request failed. Nobody was going to sue me over a lost credit, but it bothered me, so I rebuilt the billing so failed requests refund automatically. At Nutanix this summer, I worked on the cloud control plane and redesigned how partial updates were applied, so a bad request couldn't wipe out a customer's configuration. At Pitt's NLP lab, I cut experiment turnaround from four minutes to seventy-five seconds so researchers could spend their time thinking instead of waiting.

None of those are flashy. They're the kind of work you only notice when it's missing.

By the end of my career, I want my trademark to be trust. I want people on my teams to know that if I built it, it handles the bad day as well as the good one. I want to have mentored engineers who think about the person on the other end of the screen. And I'd like to spend part of my career on software for things like healthcare billing and benefits, the systems where a bug costs someone more than a few minutes.

I'm a computer science student at the University of Pittsburgh, graduating in December 2027. The $2,500 would cover a real share of my final semesters and let me focus on finishing strong instead of on how to pay for it.
