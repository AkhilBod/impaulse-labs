Akhil Bodahanapati

Perez Mayoral Trailblazer Scholarship
Describe your ultimate career or advocacy goals and how you envision making an impact
in your community.
When people find out I grew up in Cupertino, they usually assume they know my story. Good
schools, nice neighborhood, parents with tech jobs, a straight line to a computer science
degree. I understand why. From the outside, that's what it looked like.
What they don't see is everything that happened inside our house. My family dealt with health
problems that didn't go away and didn't wait for a good time. We moved more than once, and
each move meant a new school, new people, and starting over. Money got tight in ways
nobody around us would have guessed. That was its own kind of hard, because in a place like
Cupertino, struggling is something you keep to yourself.
The thing I remember most from those years isn't one big moment. It's how much time went
into just figuring things out. Who do you call? What does this letter mean? Which form is the
right one, and what happens if you get it wrong? Every system we dealt with, whether it was
medical, financial, or school, seemed built for people who already knew how it worked. If you
didn't, you were expected to figure it out alone, usually while you were already stretched thin.
That's where my idea of advocacy comes from. I don't picture standing at a podium. I picture
the person at the kitchen table at midnight, trying to understand a bill or a denial letter, who
doesn't know they have options. Most people who get lost in these systems aren't failing
because they don't care. They're failing because the information is buried, confusing, or
written for someone else.
I'm studying computer science at the University of Pittsburgh and graduating in December
2027. I chose CS partly because it was the one thing in my life that felt fair. Code either works
or it doesn't, and if you put in the effort, you can see exactly where you went wrong and fix it.
When everything else felt unstable, that mattered to me. Over time, I've realized that the same
skill can be used to make other people's lives feel a little more fair too.
My goal is to build software that helps people get through the systems that are supposed to
serve them. That could be tools that explain medical bills and insurance decisions in plain
language. It could be tools that help families find assistance programs they qualify for but
have never heard of. It could be tools that walk tenants and homeowners through their rights
before a problem turns into a crisis. The technology for this already exists. What's usually
missing is someone who has been on the other side of the kitchen table and cares about getting
it right for the people who need it most.
Your firm's work stood out to me for that reason. You represent individuals and small
businesses against HOAs and insurance companies, organizations that have far more resources
and far more knowledge of the rules. That gap in knowledge and power is exactly what I want
to work on, just with code instead of courtrooms. A good lawyer makes sure someone isn't
steamrolled because they didn't know their rights. I want to build tools that make sure people
know those rights before they ever need a lawyer.
In the short term, my focus is finishing my degree strong. My final semesters are the most
demanding part of the program, and the same pressures I've dealt with for years haven't
disappeared just because I'm close to the finish line. This scholarship would give me room to
focus on my coursework instead of worrying about how to cover it.
In the long term, I want to work in software that has a direct effect on people's everyday lives,
not just their entertainment. I want to bring the perspective of someone who has had to work
through these systems without help. I also want to be someone others can come to, whether it's
family, friends, or students coming up behind me, when they're trying to figure out the same
things I once had to figure out alone.
Trailblazing, to me, doesn't mean being the first person to do something impressive. It means
clearing a path so the next person has an easier time getting through. That's the kind of impact
I want to have, and it's the reason I'm working as hard as I am to finish what I started.

2. Largey Law Firm - Overcoming the Impossible Scholarship

Largey Law Firm: Overcoming The Impossible Scholarship
Prompt: Describe how you or someone you know has faced a traumatic event and found a way to overcome the struggle to better shape themselves.

When I was growing up in Cupertino, my family looked fine from the street. We lived in a town where everyone's parents seemed to work at Apple or a startup, and I went to schools where the main stress was whether you'd get into Stanford. I didn't talk about what was going on at home, mostly because nobody around me seemed to have anything like it going on, or if they did, they hid it as well as we did.

What was going on was that my family's health fell apart, and it didn't happen in a clean way where someone gets sick and then gets better. It dragged. There were appointments I drove to and waited through. There were nights when the house was quiet in a way that told you something was wrong before anyone said anything. I learned to read my parents' faces when they came back from a doctor. I also learned which questions not to ask.

Money got tight because of it. Medical bills don't care what zip code you live in, and Cupertino is an expensive place to have a bad year. Eventually we moved across the country to Pennsylvania. I was a teenager, and I remember being angry about it in a way that I'm a little embarrassed about now. I had friends, I knew the roads, I knew which teachers would let you turn things in late. Then I was in a new school where I didn't know anyone and it snowed in a way I'd only seen in movies.

I wish I could say I handled it well. I didn't, at first. I pulled back. I did the minimum, I kept to myself, and I told myself it didn't matter because none of this was going to be permanent anyway. It took me a while to realize that it was permanent, at least the part where my life wasn't going back to how it was.

The thing that pulled me out was small. I started taking a programming class through La Roche University while I was still in high school, mostly because it was something to do that wasn't sitting at home. I liked it more than I expected. Code is strict. It either runs or it doesn't, and when it doesn't, it tells you, sometimes rudely, exactly where you messed up. After a few years of things going wrong for reasons I couldn't see or fix, that was a relief. I could sit with a broken program for three hours and eventually it would work, and the reason it worked would be something I did.

That sounds like a neat ending. It wasn't. I got to Pitt and the hard stuff didn't stop just because I'd found something I liked. My calculus grade my first semester was a C. In my second year I got a D+ in Computer Organization and Assembly Language, which was the first time I'd ever really failed at something I cared about. The next spring was worse on paper. I was working at Pitt's Natural Language Processing lab, retaking that class, and taking two of the hardest courses in the CS major at the same time. My term GPA was a 2.8. I withdrew from a writing class because I couldn't keep everything above water.

Here's the part I'm actually proud of. I retook Computer Organization and got a B. I kept my job at the lab and cut one of our experiment runs from four minutes to seventy-five seconds. I built a project on the side called InterviewSense, an interview practice tool, and it now has more than 25,000 users. This past summer I interned at Nutanix in San Jose, about ten minutes from the house I grew up in, and my team won the intern hackathon. I drove past my old middle school on the way to work some days. It was strange in a good way.

I don't think the hard years made me a better person automatically. I think a lot of people go through things like what my family went through and come out of it more tired, not stronger, and that's fair. What changed for me is that I stopped waiting for things to go back to normal and started building something new with what was left. I got used to starting over. I got used to being bad at something for a while before being good at it. Those are the two things I lean on most now, in school and at work.

I'm a computer science major at Pitt, graduating in December 2027. My family is still dealing with some of the same things, and I still carry some of it with me. This scholarship would take a piece of the money pressure off during my last few semesters, which are the hardest ones in the program. I would put that space toward finishing the way I want to finish, which is better than I started.

3. Umansky Law Firm - Second Chance Foundation Scholarship

Umansky Law Firm: Second Chance Foundation Scholarship
Prompt: Share a hardship you have overcome and what a second chance means to you.

The first time I took CS 0447, Computer Organization and Assembly Language, I got a D+.

If you haven't taken it, 0447 is the class where Pitt CS students learn what a computer is actually doing underneath the code. You write assembly by hand, you trace how data moves through registers and memory, and you find out that a single wrong bit can make a program do something completely different from what you meant. It's a class a lot of people struggle in. I still took the grade personally. I had always been the kid who was good with computers. Seeing a D+ next to a CS course felt like someone had found me out.

I could explain that semester with a list of reasons, and some of them would be real. My family has been dealing with health problems for years. We'd already moved across the country once, from Cupertino to Pennsylvania, and money had been tight since. I was carrying a full course load and trying to keep up with everything else. But I also know that part of it was me. I didn't go to office hours. I thought I could figure it out alone the way I'd figured out most things, and I waited too long to admit I couldn't.

The D+ came with a choice. I could quietly switch majors, which honestly crossed my mind, or I could retake the class. I retook it the next spring, which turned out to be the hardest semester I've had. I was also taking Algorithms and Data Structures 2 and Formal Methods, two of the courses Pitt students warn each other about, and I had just started working at the university's Natural Language Processing lab. Something had to give, and I ended up withdrawing from a writing class to protect the rest. My term GPA that spring was a 2.8. Not great.

But I got a B in 0447 the second time.

I know a B doesn't sound like a dramatic comeback. To me it was the whole point. The second time through I went to office hours. I worked problems on paper before touching the keyboard. I stopped treating not understanding something as a sign that I didn't belong, and started treating it as a normal Tuesday. That shift mattered more than the letter grade, because it changed how I've approached everything since.

The same semester, at the NLP lab, I worked on infrastructure that ran experiments across about a million documents. Our experiment runs took four minutes each, which doesn't sound like much until you're running hundreds of them. I spent a few weeks rewriting our database queries and adding a job queue and got it down to seventy-five seconds. A year earlier I would have assumed that kind of problem was for someone smarter than me. After 0447, I assumed it was something I could figure out if I was willing to be stuck for a while first.

That summer I interned at Nutanix in San Jose, working on the backend that cloud customers depend on. One of my projects was fixing how partial updates worked, so that one bad request couldn't wipe out a customer's configuration. It's a funny thing to work on after the year I'd had. A lot of good engineering is about building systems that give you a second chance, that let you recover from a mistake without losing everything. I'd been on the receiving end of that kind of grace, and I liked being on the building end of it.

So what does a second chance mean to me? Mostly, it means somebody let me try again without deciding who I was based on my worst semester. Pitt let me retake the class. My lab took me on when my transcript wasn't perfect. My family kept believing I'd figure it out even when they had plenty of their own problems to deal with. None of those people were obligated to do that.

I'm graduating in December 2027 with a computer science degree. I still have the D+ on my transcript, marked as repeated, and I'm fine with it being there. It's an honest record of where I started. This scholarship would help me get through my last semesters, which are some of the most demanding in the major, without the money pressure that's followed my family for years. And at some point I want to be the person who gives someone else a second try, whether that's a junior engineer who breaks something in production or a student who is staring at a D+ and wondering if they should quit.

4. Easton & Easton College Scholarship

The Easton & Easton College Scholarship
Prompt: What are the top three skills you are working to cultivate that will help you achieve your career goals?

I want to be a software engineer who builds things that hold up when real people use them. I've had enough internships and side projects by now to know that writing code that works on my laptop is the easy part. The skills I'm working on are the ones that come after that.

The first is thinking about what happens when things go wrong.

When I started programming, I wrote code for the happy path. The user types the right input, the network is up, the database answers. Then I built InterviewSense, an AI interview practice platform, and real people started using it. Today it has more than 25,000 users. Real users find every edge case you didn't think about, and some you couldn't have. One early problem: we charged usage credits before calling an AI model, and if the model request failed, the user lost the credit and got nothing. Nobody complained loudly, but I noticed the support emails. I rebuilt the billing flow so failed requests automatically gave the credit back. It was a small fix, but it was the first time I thought about failure as something to design for instead of something to apologize for.

I got a bigger version of that lesson this summer at Nutanix, where I interned on the control plane for their cloud infrastructure. I worked on how partial updates were applied to customer clusters. The old logic could, in certain situations, overwrite settings a customer never meant to change. You don't notice a bug like that until it hits someone's production system. I redesigned the update path to preserve existing state, and added checks for conflicting writes when two requests hit the same resource at once. I'm still learning how to anticipate those cases before they happen. My instinct now is to ask "what's the worst thing this could do" before I ask "does it work."

The second skill is making things faster without making them fragile.

At Pitt's Natural Language Processing lab, researchers ran experiments over roughly a million documents. Each run took about four minutes, which meant people waited around a lot and ran fewer experiments than they wanted to. I dug into it and found that most of the time was spent in slow database queries and in work that could have been done in parallel. I rewrote the PostgreSQL queries and added Redis-backed job queues, and turnaround dropped to about seventy-five seconds.

What I took from that wasn't really about databases. It was that performance work is mostly patience. You measure, you find the one thing that matters, and you leave everything else alone. I used to want to rewrite whole systems. Now I'm more interested in finding the one slow line. I'm practicing this in my coursework too, especially in Database Management Systems this semester, where I can see why the tricks I used at the lab worked.

The third skill is communicating clearly, and I'll be honest that this is the one I'm furthest behind on.

I'm better at talking to computers than to people. In my sophomore spring, I withdrew from a professional writing course because I was overloaded, and I knew at the time that I was dropping the class I probably needed most. I'm retaking a professional communication course this semester and taking it seriously. At work I've started to notice how much good engineering depends on writing. A design doc that's clear saves a week of meetings. A pull request description that explains the "why" saves a reviewer an hour. At Federated Hermes I worked directly with portfolio managers to replace manual workflows with backend services, and the hard part was never the Python. It was understanding what they actually needed and explaining what I could build in words that made sense to someone who doesn't write code.

I've also seen what happens when communication goes well. At the Nutanix intern hackathon, my team built a Kubernetes scheduler that automatically parked idle GPUs, and we won for the US and EMEA region. The project was good, but I think we won because we explained it in two minutes in a way that made judges care about GPU waste. I want to be able to do that on purpose, not by accident.

These three skills fit together for me. I want to build systems that survive mistakes, that are fast enough people actually use them, and that other people can understand and trust. I'm graduating from the University of Pittsburgh with a computer science degree in December 2027. After that I want to work on infrastructure and backend systems, the kind of software that most people never see but that everything depends on.

My family has dealt with health problems and financial strain for a long time, including a move across the country from California to Pennsylvania. Those years taught me to work hard without assuming things would go smoothly. This scholarship would help cover my last semesters so I can spend more time getting better at these skills and less time worrying about how to pay for them.

7. Dundee Deco Scholarship

Dundee Deco Scholarship (Google Form, 300 words)
Topic: The Psychology of Space: how study environment design affects academic performance and mental health

The worst semester I've had at Pitt, I did almost all of my studying in bed.

It made sense at the time. I was taking a heavy course load, retaking a class I'd done badly in, and working at a research lab, so I was tired constantly, and my bed was the closest flat surface. What I didn't notice until later was that my brain stopped separating work from rest. I'd lie down to sleep and start thinking about an algorithm. I'd sit down to study and feel like napping. My grades that spring were the lowest they've been.

The next semester I changed one thing. I set up a small desk by the window, put a lamp on it with warm light instead of my overhead light, and made a rule that the bed was only for sleeping. Having a separate place for work meant I could actually leave work when I left the desk. I slept better, and I focused faster when I sat down because my brain knew what that chair was for.

I think this is the core of how space affects students: environments teach habits. Natural light helps people stay alert and keep a regular sleep schedule. A cluttered desk competes for attention, even when you aren't looking at it. Even small boundaries, like a specific chair or a specific lamp, can give your brain a cue that it's time to focus.

Most student housing isn't designed with any of this in mind. Rooms are small, lighting is harsh, and the bed is often the only comfortable seat. Better design wouldn't need to be expensive. It needs to give students a place that means work and a different place that means rest.

I learned that the hard way. Good design could teach it earlier.
