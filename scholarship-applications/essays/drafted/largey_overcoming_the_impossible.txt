Largey Law Firm: Overcoming The Impossible Scholarship
Prompt: Describe how you or someone you know has faced a traumatic event and found a way to overcome the struggle to better shape themselves.

When I was growing up in Cupertino, my family looked fine from the street. We lived in a town where everyone's parents seemed to work at Apple or a startup, and I went to schools where the main stress was whether you'd get into Stanford. I didn't talk about what was going on at home, mostly because nobody around me seemed to have anything like it going on, or if they did, they hid it as well as we did.

What was going on was that my family's health fell apart, and it didn't happen in a clean way where someone gets sick and then gets better. It dragged. There were appointments I drove to and waited through. There were nights when the house was quiet in a way that told you something was wrong before anyone said anything. I learned to read my parents' faces when they came back from a doctor. I also learned which questions not to ask.

Money got tight because of it. Medical bills don't care what zip code you live in, and Cupertino is an expensive place to have a bad year. Eventually we moved across the country to Pennsylvania. I was a teenager, and I remember being angry about it in a way that I'm a little embarrassed about now. I had friends, I knew the roads, I knew which teachers would let you turn things in late. Then I was in a new school where I didn't know anyone and it snowed in a way I'd only seen in movies.

I wish I could say I handled it well. I didn't, at first. I pulled back. I did the minimum, I kept to myself, and I told myself it didn't matter because none of this was going to be permanent anyway. It took me a while to realize that it was permanent, at least the part where my life wasn't going back to how it was.

The thing that pulled me out was small. I started taking a programming class through La Roche University while I was still in high school, mostly because it was something to do that wasn't sitting at home. I liked it more than I expected. Code is strict. It either runs or it doesn't, and when it doesn't, it tells you, sometimes rudely, exactly where you messed up. After a few years of things going wrong for reasons I couldn't see or fix, that was a relief. I could sit with a broken program for three hours and eventually it would work, and the reason it worked would be something I did.

That sounds like a neat ending. It wasn't. I got to Pitt and the hard stuff didn't stop just because I'd found something I liked. My calculus grade my first semester was a C. In my second year I got a D+ in Computer Organization and Assembly Language, which was the first time I'd ever really failed at something I cared about. The next spring was worse on paper. I was working at Pitt's Natural Language Processing lab, retaking that class, and taking two of the hardest courses in the CS major at the same time. My term GPA was a 2.8. I withdrew from a writing class because I couldn't keep everything above water.

Here's the part I'm actually proud of. I retook Computer Organization and got a B. I kept my job at the lab and cut one of our experiment runs from four minutes to seventy-five seconds. I built a project on the side called InterviewSense, an interview practice tool, and it now has more than 25,000 users. This past summer I interned at Nutanix in San Jose, about ten minutes from the house I grew up in, and my team won the intern hackathon. I drove past my old middle school on the way to work some days. It was strange in a good way.

I don't think the hard years made me a better person automatically. I think a lot of people go through things like what my family went through and come out of it more tired, not stronger, and that's fair. What changed for me is that I stopped waiting for things to go back to normal and started building something new with what was left. I got used to starting over. I got used to being bad at something for a while before being good at it. Those are the two things I lean on most now, in school and at work.

I'm a computer science major at Pitt, graduating in December 2027. My family is still dealing with some of the same things, and I still carry some of it with me. This scholarship would take a piece of the money pressure off during my last few semesters, which are the hardest ones in the program. I would put that space toward finishing the way I want to finish, which is better than I started.
